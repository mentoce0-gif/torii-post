There's a particular kind of unease that comes with your first AmiAmi preorder. You've reserved a figure that ships in fourteen months, the confirmation email says nothing about payment, and your card hasn't been touched. Weeks pass. Nothing happens. And somewhere in the back of your mind, a question forms: *is this actually a real order?*

It is. AmiAmi's preorder system simply works on a model that Western storefronts have largely abandoned — you commit first and pay at the end, rather than paying up front and waiting. That's genuinely better for you in most respects. It also means the risky moments sit in completely different places than you'd expect, and the two that matter most arrive with almost no warning after a year of silence.

This guide covers exactly when money moves, exactly when you can still back out, and the mistakes that turn an ordinary preorder into a closed account. Everything here comes from AmiAmi's own help pages, cited as I go, because the collector forums are full of confident advice on this topic that their published policy doesn't support.

## You Are Not Charged When You Preorder

This is the part that trips up anyone arriving from Western preorder culture, where a reservation usually means an immediate charge or at minimum a card authorisation.

AmiAmi's position is plain: **pre-payment is not required for preorder items** unless the product page says otherwise. Their "When to Pay" page notes only two exceptions — they may request pre-payment for large orders, or for certain items such as high-priced or made-to-order goods. Everything else, you reserve for free.

So when you place a preorder in March for an item releasing next May, nothing leaves your account in March. Your card details may sit on file, but they are not charged. What you've made is a commitment, not a transaction.

Two consequences follow, and both are worth internalising.

The first is that **you carry no financial risk during the wait**, which is a considerable stretch of time in this hobby. Scale figures routinely take twelve to eighteen months from announcement to release, and delays push that further. During all of it, your money stays in your account rather than sitting with a retailer.

The second is that **the entire cost of the order lands at once, months after you decided to buy it.** People forget preorders. They forget them in a way that's easy to laugh about until three of them come due in the same month, which happens more often than chance suggests — figure releases cluster around the same shipping windows. The single most useful habit in this hobby is keeping a list of what you've reserved and roughly when each is due, because AmiAmi will not remind you until the invoice arrives, and by then the clock has started.

## What Actually Triggers the Invoice

Here's the mechanism, in AmiAmi's own words:

> We generally invoice our customers after all items in an order are available at our warehouse and the total shipping fee is calculated.

Read that carefully, because the important word is *all*.

An order is the unit AmiAmi bills. Not an item — an order. If your order contains four things and three of them are sitting on a shelf in Japan while the fourth is delayed to next quarter, **nothing is invoiced and nothing ships.** Their help centre confirms it directly: if your order mixes preorder and in-stock items, the order is invoiced after all preorder items have been released.

This is the single most common way people get frustrated with AmiAmi, and it isn't a fault in the system so much as a consequence of how the system is built. You added a keychain to a preorder to make the shipping feel more worthwhile. That keychain is now hostage to a figure whose release slipped twice.

When everything is finally ready, AmiAmi calculates shipping and sends a **"Payment Request" email** containing a payment link to the address registered on your account. That email is the starting gun for everything in the next section.

It's also the first time you see what shipping costs, which matters more than it sounds — AmiAmi states it cannot estimate shipping for pre-orders at all, because the item's weight and dimensions don't exist until it's made. If you're in the United States that gap is wider still, since every Japan Post option was withdrawn in 2025 and what replaced it prices differently. I've set out [what AmiAmi shipping to the US actually costs, and why nobody can quote you](/guides/amiami-shipping-to-the-us/) separately.

AmiAmi says only that they send invoices "as quickly as possible" and that it "may take some time." The specific figures circulating on collector forums — one to two days, seven to ten business days — trace back to threads written during the pandemic backlog, five years ago now. There's a related trap worth knowing. AmiAmi will only combine orders under specific conditions: in-stock with in-stock, in-stock with a preorder releasing *this* month, or preorders releasing in the *same* month. Items scheduled for different months cannot be combined. AmiAmi openly admits their system sometimes lets you combine things it shouldn't — particularly at the start of a month, or when a release has been delayed — and asks you not to. And if a release date change splits your order, the pieces can't be re-joined unless they happen to land in the same month again.

## The Seven-Day Clock

Once the Payment Request email is sent, you have **seven days from the date of the first invoice**. AmiAmi's Payment Deadline page is unusually direct about the limits around it:

- They cannot accept split payments.
- They cannot extend the deadline.
- **Combining orders does not extend your payment deadline.**

That last one deserves emphasis, because it's the opposite of what people intuitively try. When the invoice lands at an awkward moment, the instinct is to combine it with a later order and buy time. It doesn't work, and attempting to manipulate the deadline through address or shipping-method changes is the kind of behaviour that draws attention to your account rather than solving the problem.

Now the part that genuinely surprised me, and the reason this article exists:

> any orders that have gone past the stated 7-day payment period will be canceled and any items contained within will be **ineligible to be re-ordered at our store**.

Missing your payment window does not simply cancel the order and return you to where you started. According to AmiAmi's own wording, **those items become ineligible for you to order again.** For a mass-produced figure with open preorders, that may be a shrug. For a limited exclusive you waited a year for, it is the end of the road at retail — you'll be paying aftermarket prices for something you had reserved.

I've seen this policy described nowhere else in English. Most guides stop at "your order will be cancelled," which is true and importantly incomplete.

The practical implication is simple and worth acting on: **the email address on your AmiAmi account must be one you actually read, and it must be able to receive mail from amiami.com.** Their list of reasons orders get cancelled opens with exactly this — emails not reaching the customer, blocked by an ISP, a spam filter, or an email client. A preorder you waited fourteen months for can die in a spam folder. Add their domain to your allowlist now, not when it matters.

## What You Can and Can't Cancel

AmiAmi allows cancellation through a "Request Cancellation" page in My Account. The word *request* is doing real work there — this is not a self-service cancel button.

| Order state | Can you request cancellation? |
|---|---|
| Preorder, not yet released | **Yes** |
| Preorder, after the order has been invoiced | **No** |
| In-stock or pre-owned item, before the payment deadline | **Yes** (see caveat below) |
| Back-order item | **No** |
| Paid order | **No** |
| Shipped order | **No** |
| Locked order, or one with shipping restrictions | **No** |

A few mechanics around that table matter as much as the table itself.

**It's reviewed, and you hear back within one business day.** Notifications may be slower across weekends and Japanese public holidays. If your order shows as "Processing…" you already have a request in flight and can't submit another until it resolves.

**It cannot be undone.** AmiAmi's answer to "Can I undo a cancellation request?" is a single sentence: *"Unfortunately, cancellation requests cannot be undone."* There is no grace period and no second thoughts. Decide before you submit.

**Quantity 2 or more cancels entirely.** If you ordered three of something and submit a cancellation request, all three are cancelled — the form has no quantity control. Cancelling part of a multiple requires contacting support directly instead.

**The reason you type in does not affect the decision.** This one is worth dwelling on, because it contradicts the most common piece of folk advice about AmiAmi. The cancellation form requires a reason, and their page guide states plainly that the reason "will not be used for determine if a request will be accepted or not." They ask you to be accurate, but a well-crafted explanation earns you nothing. What decides the outcome is the item's status, and nothing else — their guide closes by saying that as long as your request is in accordance with the cancellation policy, they will cancel the requested items.

So the honest summary is: **eligibility is the gate, not persuasion.** If the item qualifies, you're fine. If it doesn't, no amount of politeness moves it.

<div class="callout"><span class="callout-label">One caveat on in-stock items</span><p>Being able to cancel in-stock and pre-owned items is described on AmiAmi's own page guide as a COVID-era measure, still flagged as running "until further notice" with an update note from June 2023. It has held for years and shows no sign of changing. But it is written as a temporary concession rather than a permanent right, so I wouldn't build a buying habit that depends on it.</p></div>

## Where the Window Actually Closes — and Why It's Unclear

Here is something I didn't expect to find: **AmiAmi's two help pages on cancellation don't say the same thing.**

| Help page | Where it says the window closes |
|---|---|
| *How Can I Request Order Cancellation?* | Cancellations are not accepted for preorder items **after a payment request email has been sent** |
| *How can I request to cancel an item/order?* | Preorders may be cancelled **up until their release date**; not including preorders **after the order has been invoiced** |

At a glance these look like the same rule stated twice. They aren't, and the gap between them is exactly where a lot of real orders live.

Remember the billing mechanic: an order isn't invoiced until *every* item in it is ready. So picture an order with two preorders — one released in August, one delayed to November. From August onwards you're holding a **preorder that has been released but has not been invoiced**, and will not be for months.

The first page says the trigger is the payment request, which hasn't happened. That reading says you can still cancel. The second page says preorders can be cancelled up until their release date, which has passed. That reading says you can't.

**AmiAmi doesn't resolve this anywhere I could find.** I'm not going to guess on their behalf, and I'd treat any guide that states this confidently as making it up.

What I'd actually do: **treat the release date as your deadline.** If you're going to cancel a preorder, do it before the item comes out, and you never have to care which reading is correct. If you're already past release and still uninvoiced, submit the request — it costs nothing and you'll have an answer in a business day — but don't count on it.

## What Actually Gets Accounts Restricted

Every guide mentions that excessive cancellations are frowned upon. Fewer explain what AmiAmi has actually written down, which is more specific and slightly more alarming.

Their cancellation page guide states that **the store does not allow "placeholder orders"**, and that if misuse of the cancellation system or excessive cancellations are detected, your account may be subject to restrictions. That's the mechanism people mean when they say "don't cancel too much." Reserving things you haven't decided on, intending to cancel later, is the specific behaviour they're describing.

Their separate page on account closures lists what most commonly leads to an account being closed temporarily or permanently:

- A long history of unpaid orders, or cancellations not allowed by store policy
- Opening a claim or chargeback through a third party against an order that has already shipped
- Creating multiple accounts, or sharing an address with other users
- Other behaviour that causes damage to their business

Two of those catch ordinary people who weren't trying to game anything.

**Chargebacks.** If a parcel goes missing, the instinct is to call your card issuer. Doing that on an order AmiAmi has already shipped is on their list of account-closing behaviour. Contact them first and let their process run — that's what it exists for.

**Shared addresses.** Two collectors in the same household with separate accounts, or asking a friend to pay for your order because your card is failing, can read as duplicate accounts. AmiAmi's own answer on this is blunt: accounts may be determined to be duplicates and **both** may be closed without warning. If you're in that situation, contact support before you do it, not after.

There's also a purchase-limit dimension. High-demand items carry per-customer limits, and repeatedly exceeding them can close an account and cancel open orders. Note that **already-shipped items count towards the limit** unless the product page says otherwise — so a second order months later can still breach a limit you thought you'd left behind.

One reassurance to balance all that: a temporarily closed account is re-opened once the underlying issue is resolved. Temporary and permanent closure are different outcomes, and most problems land in the first category.

AmiAmi has never published a threshold, and anyone quoting you one is guessing. What I can tell you is the shape of the rule rather than its size: they're looking for a <em>pattern</em> — placeholder reservations and habitual cancellations — not for individual instances. ## How to Preorder So None of This Bites

Everything above collapses into a handful of habits.

**Keep preorders in their own orders.** The temptation to bundle is strong, and shipping consolidation is a genuine saving. But every item you add to a preorder is an item that can't ship until the slowest thing in the box is ready. If you're going to combine, combine things releasing in the same month — which is also the only combination AmiAmi's rules actually permit.

**Allowlist amiami.com today.** The seven-day clock starts whether or not you saw the email, and missing it can cost you the ability to re-order the item at all. This is a two-minute job that protects a fourteen-month wait.

**Write down what you've reserved and when it's due.** AmiAmi's My Account page will show you, but the reserve-now-pay-later model means your future self is committing money your present self won't think about. A list is enough.

**Decide before you submit a cancellation.** It can't be reversed, and it cancels the full quantity of that item.

**Cancel before release, not after.** It keeps you clear of the ambiguity in their own documentation.

**Never open a chargeback on a shipped order without talking to them first.** It's on the list of things that closes accounts.

## FAQ

**Does AmiAmi charge my card automatically when the item releases?**
No. They send a Payment Request email with a payment link, and you complete payment yourself within seven days. The exception is domestic Japanese orders set to certain shipping and payment configurations, which process automatically — not something overseas buyers encounter.

**Can I pay for the in-stock part of my order now and the preorder later?**
No. AmiAmi does not accept split payments, and the order is invoiced as a whole once everything in it is available.

**What happens if I just don't pay?**
The order is cancelled after seven days, and AmiAmi states the items within it become ineligible for you to re-order at their store. It also contributes to a history of unpaid orders, which appears on their list of reasons accounts get closed.

**Can I change my mind after submitting a cancellation request?**
No. Cancellation requests cannot be undone.

**How long does a cancellation request take?**
You should receive an email about the outcome within one business day. Weekends and Japanese holidays can delay it.

**Will a good explanation improve my chances?**
No. AmiAmi's own page guide says the reason given is not used to determine whether the request is accepted. Eligibility depends on the item's status.

**Can I cancel just one of the three I ordered?**
Not through the form — submitting a request cancels the entire quantity. Contact support directly if you need a partial cancellation.

**Is a preorder ever cancelled by AmiAmi?**
Yes, and not always for anything you did. The manufacturer can cancel a release outright, or a supply shortage can leave AmiAmi unable to fulfil orders. They also cancel items that can't legally ship internationally — paints, thinners, spray cans, and certain categories like furniture and model guns. You're notified when this happens.

**Does any of this apply to pre-owned items?**
Pre-owned items are in-stock items, so they're invoiced immediately rather than waiting on a release, and cancellation is possible up to the payment deadline under the caveat noted above. If you're weighing up a pre-owned listing, the grading system is a separate topic worth understanding first — I've written about [what AmiAmi's pre-owned grades actually mean](/guides/amiami-preowned-grades-explained/) in practice.

## The Bottom Line

AmiAmi's preorder model is genuinely customer-friendly in the way that matters most: your money stays with you for the year or more that you're waiting, and backing out before release is a normal, permitted thing to do rather than a fight.

What it asks in return is attention at two specific moments. The first is the Payment Request email, which starts a seven-day clock that cannot be paused, extended, or worked around, and whose penalty is harsher than most people realise. The second is the decision to cancel, which is one-way, quantity-wide, and judged purely on whether the item qualifies.

Get those two right and the rest of the system will treat you well for years.
