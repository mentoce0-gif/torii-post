<p><strong>Hakone is the hot spring you can do between breakfast and dinner in Tokyo.</strong> Ninety minutes on the train, a bath in the mountains, home the same night — no ryokan booking, no overnight bag. The part nobody costs out honestly is what you actually spend once you're there, and which baths will turn you away at the door.</p>

<p>Four day-use baths, ¥900 to ¥15,400. Here's the real arithmetic.</p>

## The short version

Hakone works as a day trip because the geography cooperates. Hakone-Yumoto, the gateway station, is 85–90 minutes from Shinjuku, and three of the four baths below are within a few minutes of that station — one of them is literally a three-minute walk uphill. You do not need to penetrate deep into the mountains to soak in real hot spring water.

What you do need to decide, before you buy any ticket, is which of two trips you're taking:

- **The bath trip.** Train to Hakone-Yumoto, soak, eat, train home. You will not use the ropeway, the pirate ship, or the mountain buses. **You almost certainly should not buy the Hakone Free Pass.**
- **The loop trip.** The full Hakone circuit — mountain railway, cable car, ropeway over the sulphur vents at Owakudani, boat across Lake Ashi — with a bath at the end. **Here the Free Pass pays for itself easily.**

Most guides sell you the Free Pass on the assumption you're doing the second trip. If you're doing the first, it's a waste of about ¥3,000. We'll do that math properly below.

## The Free Pass math, done honestly

The <a href="https://www.klook.com/en-US/activity/821-hakone-transport-day-pass-tokyo/?aid=129411" rel="sponsored noopener" target="_blank">Hakone Free Pass</a> is Odakyu's regional ticket: unlimited travel on eight transport networks inside Hakone, plus the round trip from Shinjuku to Odawara. It costs **¥7,100 for two days from Shinjuku** (¥7,500 for three), or **¥6,000 for two days if you only want the Hakone-area portion** and are getting to Odawara some other way.

Note what it does *not* include: the Romancecar, Odakyu's reserved limited express. That's the comfortable train with the panoramic front windows, and it's **an extra ¥1,200 each way** from Shinjuku. The Free Pass gets you on the regular express for free; the Romancecar is a seat reservation on top.

Now the comparison. A plain return ticket Shinjuku–Hakone-Yumoto runs roughly ¥1,270 each way — call it **¥2,540 return**.

- **Bath trip:** ¥2,540 in transport. The Free Pass would cost ¥7,100 to save you nothing, because you're not riding anything else. **Skip it.**
- **Loop trip:** the mountain railway, cable car, ropeway and lake boat, bought separately, comfortably exceed ¥4,000 on their own — and that's before the return fare. **Buy it.**

The honest summary: the Free Pass is excellent value for the sightseeing circuit and poor value for a bath run. Which one you're doing is a question about your day, not about the ticket.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: the pass gets you **discounts at over 70 facilities**, and Yunessun is one of them. If Yunessun is your bath and you're doing the loop anyway, that discount narrows the gap further.

## 1. Kappa Tengoku — the cheapest real onsen near a Tokyo train

Three minutes on foot from Hakone-Yumoto station, up a short slope behind it, is an open-air bath that costs **¥900**. There's no shuttle to wait for and no bus to work out. You walk off the train and you're basically there.

It is not luxurious. It's an outdoor rock pool under the trees, a bit weathered, frequented by locals and day-trippers who know it's there. That's the appeal — it's the least packaged hot spring experience in easy reach of Tokyo, and it costs less than lunch.

**The caveat you need to hear:** Kappa Tengoku's official website publishes its opening hours and its location, and essentially nothing else. **No prices. No tattoo policy.** The ¥900 figure comes from the Hakone Tourist Association rather than the facility itself, and while it's consistently reported, we're flagging it as second-hand. If you have tattoos, we can't tell you what will happen at the door, because the facility has never said — **call 0460-85-6121 before you go.**

**Go if:** you want a genuine outdoor onsen with zero logistics and don't need it to be pretty.

## 2. Tenzan Toji-kyo — the one with the rule nobody translates

Tenzan is the local favourite: a sprawling complex of stone-lined outdoor pools at different temperatures, set in a wooded valley a short ride from Hakone-Yumoto. **¥1,450**, open **9:00 to 23:00** — the longest hours of anywhere on this list, which makes it the answer if your morning went sideways and you're arriving at four in the afternoon.

Two practical warnings that catch foreign visitors:

**It's cash only.** The facility states this plainly. Bring ¥1,450 in actual notes and coins; there is no card fallback.

**The tattoo rule is unusual, and no English guide seems to mention it.** Tenzan does admit tattooed guests. But its own policy states that only *one* tattooed person may enter at a time. In its own words, the facility will not refuse you for having a tattoo, but use is limited to "one person only" — and if two or more people in a group have tattoos, regardless of gender, **the group will not be admitted.**

Read that again if you're travelling with a tattooed friend, because it's the difference between a nice afternoon and being turned around at reception. The stated reasoning is about avoiding a sense of intimidation among other bathers when several tattooed guests are present together. Whatever you make of that logic, it's the rule, and it's published.

**Go if:** you want the best pools on this list, you have cash, and at most one of you has ink.

## 3. Hakone Yuryo — the one that changed its mind about tattoos

Hakone Yuryo is the modern, deliberately handsome option: dark wood, paper lanterns, a valley setting, and a **free shuttle that runs every 10–15 minutes from Hakone-Yumoto and takes about three minutes.** Main baths are ¥1,700–1,800 on weekdays and ¥2,000–2,200 at weekends.

The reason it leads this section, though, is a policy change that hasn't reached most English-language coverage yet: **Hakone Yuryo lifted its tattoo restrictions entirely on 1 April 2025**, and says so on its own English website — framing it as a response to changing values and cultural diversity.

That matters more than it sounds. The standard workaround for tattooed visitors in Japan has always been to rent a private bath, because communal bathing was closed to you. At Hakone Yuryo, **the communal baths are now open too.** For a first-rate facility ninety minutes from Tokyo, that's the single most useful sentence in this article for a lot of readers. If that's your situation, our [guide to bathing with tattoos in Japan](/guides/onsen-with-tattoos-japan/) covers the wider picture.

### The private rooms, and the number everyone gets wrong

Yuryo's *Hanare Yuya Kaden* wing holds **19 private open-air baths** — one of the largest collections anywhere near Tokyo. This is where you go if you want an outdoor tub behind a locked door with nobody else in it.

Be clear on how it's priced, because the internet is full of stale figures, including an older version of this site's own guide:

| Room | Capacity | Weekday / 120 min | Weekend / 120 min |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ichi-no-maki | 2 people | ¥10,400 | ¥11,400 |
| Ni-no-maki | 4 people | ¥12,400 | ¥13,400 |
| San-no-maki | 4 people | ¥14,400 | ¥15,400 |

**There is no hourly rate and no shorter slot — 120 minutes is the minimum booking.** Extensions run in 30-minute blocks from ¥2,600. The main bathhouse is a separate charge on top if you want both: ¥1,000 per adult on weekdays, ¥1,300 at weekends. You can also skip the main baths entirely and just use your room.

Reservations open a month ahead by phone, and **from 17 August 2026 you can book online** rather than calling. If you're reading this after that date, that's your easiest path. For the wider question of reserving private baths across Japan in English, we've covered that in [our private onsen guide](/guides/private-onsen-japan-book-in-english/).

**Go if:** you want the polished version, or you have tattoos and want a proper communal soak without negotiating.

## 4. Yunessun — the water park that isn't really an onsen trip

Hakone Kowakien Yunessun is the famous one — the place with the wine bath and the coffee bath, where you wear a swimsuit and wander between themed pools. It's genuinely fun with kids. It is not a quiet soak.

It splits into two halves, ticketed separately or together:

- **Yunessun**, the swimsuit zone: **¥2,500–3,000** for adults
- **Mori no Yu**, the traditional nude bathing zone: **¥1,500–1,800**
- **Passport**, both: **¥3,500–4,000**

Those ranges aren't hedging. Yunessun uses **variable pricing across three tiers**, so the price on a quiet weekday is genuinely lower than on a summer Saturday. Anyone quoting you a single figure is quoting one tier and not telling you. Tickets are also <a href="https://www.klook.com/en-US/activity/13954-hakone-kowaki-en-yunessun-hot-spring-theme-park-one-day-ticket-tokyo/?aid=129411" rel="sponsored noopener" target="_blank">sold in advance on Klook</a> with English checkout, which is the simplest route if you'd rather not work out the tier system at the gate.

**On tattoos, be careful here.** Several English guides claim the swimsuit zone is tattoo-friendly on the logic that you're clothed anyway. **That is not what the facility says.** Yunessun's official position is that guests with tattoos — including small ones — are refused entry in principle. In the swimsuit zone you may enter if the tattoo is covered by a rash guard or similar clothing. In Mori no Yu, you may enter only if it's completely covered by **two of the facility's designated tattoo-concealing seals**, which you buy on site.

So: coverable, conditionally. Not "tattoo-friendly." If you have a large piece, this is not your day trip — take the twenty-minute bus ride back and go to Hakone Yuryo instead.

**Go if:** you have children, or you want novelty over tranquility, and nobody in the group needs to cover up.

## How to actually use the bath

If this is your first Japanese hot spring, the sequence matters more than the etiquette lectures suggest. Here's what the twenty minutes after you pay look like.

**Shoes come off at the entrance**, before you reach the front desk — there's a rack or a locker for them. Then you pay, and at day-use places like these you're usually handed a wristband or a locker key rather than a ticket.

**The changing room is split by gender**, marked with the characters 男 (men) and 女 (women), usually blue and red respectively. Inside, you undress completely. This is the part first-timers stall on: swimsuits are not worn in Japanese hot springs — the sole exception on this list is Yunessun's swimsuit zone, where one is mandatory. Everything goes in the locker.

**You take one small towel in with you.** It never touches the water. Fold it on your head, which is what everyone else is doing, or leave it on the rim.

**Wash before you get in.** There's a row of seated shower stations with a stool, a hand shower, and soap. Sit, wash properly, rinse off every trace of suds. The communal bath is for soaking in clean water, not for cleaning yourself — this is the one rule where getting it wrong is genuinely rude rather than merely foreign.

**Tie long hair up** so it stays out of the water.

Then get in slowly. Hakone's water typically runs 40–42°C, which is hotter than most Western baths, and ten to fifteen minutes is a normal stretch before you get out to cool down. **Drink water afterwards** — you'll have sweated more than you think, and light-headedness on the walk back to the station is the commonest way a good soak turns unpleasant.

Don't rinse in the shower on the way out if you'd rather keep the minerals on your skin. Plenty of regulars don't.

## Building the actual day

A shape that works, using the bath trip (no Free Pass):

- **08:30** Shinjuku. Regular express to Hakone-Yumoto, ~90 minutes, about ¥1,270. Pay the ¥1,200 Romancecar supplement if you want the good seats and the view.
- **10:00** Arrive. Drop bags in a station coin locker — none of these baths want your luggage.
- **10:15** Bath. Yuryo's shuttle leaves from outside the station; Kappa Tengoku you simply walk to; Tenzan is a short ride.
- **12:30** Lunch along the Hakone-Yumoto main street, which is one long row of soba, seafood bowls and steamed buns.
- **14:00** Second soak, or the Hakone Tozan railway two stops up the mountain and back for the switchback ride, which is worth doing and cheap.
- **17:00** Train back. Home in Tokyo for dinner.

If you're doing the loop version instead, invert it: circuit first, bath last, and buy the Free Pass. A hot spring at the end of the loop is a much better day than a hot spring at the start of one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I do Hakone as a day trip from Tokyo?

Yes, comfortably. Hakone-Yumoto is 85–90 minutes from Shinjuku, and the baths nearest the station are a three-minute walk or a three-minute shuttle away. A 9am departure gets you soaking by 10:30 and home for dinner.

### Do I need the Hakone Free Pass?

Only if you're doing the sightseeing loop — the mountain railway, ropeway and lake boat. For a straight bath trip, a return ticket is about ¥2,540 against ¥7,100 for the pass, and you'd use none of what the pass unlocks.

### Which Hakone onsen allows tattoos?

Hakone Yuryo, without conditions, since April 2025 — communal baths included. Tenzan admits tattooed guests but only one per group. Yunessun refuses tattoos in principle and requires them covered. Kappa Tengoku has no published policy, so call ahead.

### Do I need to book a Hakone day-use onsen in advance?

Not for the communal baths — all four take walk-ins. Private rooms at Hakone Yuryo are a different matter: 19 rooms, a 120-minute minimum booking, and weekends sell out. Reserve those up to a month ahead.

### Is there an onsen right at Hakone-Yumoto station?

Kappa Tengoku is about three minutes on foot from the station — the closest genuine hot spring to a Tokyo-bound train. Hakone Yuryo runs a free shuttle from the station forecourt that takes about the same time.

### Can I visit an onsen with a small child?

Check first. Hakone Yuryo does not admit children below elementary school age to its main bathhouse, and steers families toward the private rooms instead. Yunessun's swimsuit zone is the most child-friendly option in Hakone by a distance.

## The short answer

If you have tattoos, go to **Hakone Yuryo** — it's the one that changed its rules, and it changed them properly. If you want the best water for the least money, go to **Tenzan**, bring cash, and check how many of you have ink. If you want to spend ¥900 and be soaking twenty minutes after your train arrives, walk up the hill to **Kappa Tengoku**. If you've got kids, **Yunessun** is the obvious call and nobody else's opinion matters.

And unless you're riding the ropeway, don't buy the Free Pass. That one decision is worth more than every other tip here.
