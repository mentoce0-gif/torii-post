There is a particular kind of research spiral that only tattooed travelers to Japan know. You search "onsen tattoo," you find a listicle, the listicle names ten places, you check one of them and its website says nothing at all, and you close the tab no more certain than when you opened it.

The reason that spiral never ends is that most guides answer the wrong question. They tell you *whether* tattooed people can bathe in Japan — a question with a boring answer — instead of telling you *which specific door you can walk through on Tuesday*, and *how confident anyone should be about that*.

This guide is the second thing. Eleven places, sorted by how well documented each policy is, because "a bathhouse says so on its own website" and "a blog said so in 2019" are not the same claim and shouldn't be printed in the same font.

## The Rule Nobody Actually Wrote Down

Start with what the rule is not. There is no Japanese law about tattoos and bathing. The Ministry of Health does not maintain a list. Nobody is going to arrest you.

What exists instead is a private-sector convention, inherited rather than designed. For most of the twentieth century, the people with large tattoos in a Japanese bathhouse were overwhelmingly members of organized crime, and a "no tattoos" sign was a cheap way for a small business to keep the local syndicate from turning the changing room into an office. The sign worked, the reason for it faded, and the sign stayed up.

Two things have been eroding it since the mid-2010s. Tourism authorities began nudging operators to treat foreign visitors' tattoos differently from domestic ones, on the practical grounds that a Norwegian with a shoulder piece is not a gangster. And a generation of bathhouse owners inherited their buildings from parents who had never met a customer with a sleeve tattoo who wasn't a threat.

The result is a genuinely mixed landscape, which is annoying but also the good news: it's mixed because it's moving. The places listed below are not loopholes. They're businesses that decided, on purpose, to write a different sign.

## Your Three Routes

Almost every solution falls into one of three buckets, and knowing which one you're in saves a lot of searching.

**Route 1: Go where tattoos are simply allowed.** No stickers, no negotiating, no lowering yourself into the water at an angle. This is the whole point of the list below, and it's a bigger list than most travelers expect.

**Route 2: Cover it.** Waterproof cover seals work for small, isolated tattoos at facilities whose rule is "nothing visible." They do nothing at facilities whose rule is "no tattooed guests," and they are useless for anything larger than a playing card. More on the real limits below.

**Route 3: Rent the room.** Book a private bath — *kashikiri* — and the policy question usually evaporates, because there is nobody to be uncomfortable. It's the most reliable fallback in Japan, and it works at resorts that would never let you into the main bath.

One caveat, because "private bath means the rules don't apply" is repeated everywhere and isn't quite true: **some facilities enforce their tattoo policy at the front door, not at the bath.** Solaniwa Onsen in Osaka is the clearest example — its policy requires every tattoo visible from your yukata to be covered with stickers it sells, and that applies while you are anywhere inside the building, private room booked or not. Check whether the rule is written about *the bath* or about *the facility* before you rely on this. We wrote the full guide separately: [private onsen you can book in English](/guides/private-onsen-japan-book-in-english/), and if you are considering a ryokan room with its own bath as the permanent fix, [what to check before you pay extra for one](/guides/ryokan-private-onsen-bath-what-to-check/).

Most trips end up using two of the three. You take the tattoo-friendly town for the real experience, and keep a private bath in your pocket for the night you end up somewhere else.

## Tier 1: Published, and Confirmed in Practice

### Kinosaki Onsen — the town that decided as a town

If you only remember one name from this page, make it this one. Kinosaki is a small onsen town on the Sea of Japan side of Hyogo, about two and a half hours from Kyoto, and it did something no other onsen destination has done: it aligned every public bathhouse behind a single policy instead of leaving each one to decide.

All seven *soto-yu* — Sato-no-yu, Jizo-yu, Yanagi-yu, Ichino-yu, Gosho-no-yu, Mandara-yu and Ko-no-yu — admit tattoos of any size, with no requirement to cover anything. The town's official tourism site states it plainly rather than burying it in an FAQ, which is itself a signal: a place that is quietly tolerant doesn't advertise.

**Two of the seven are shut as of August 2026, so plan for five.** Sato-no-yu has been closed for renovations since April 2024 with no announced reopening, and Ko-no-yu is closed from 11 May to 30 October 2026. The tattoo policy is unchanged at the others, but the "walk all seven in a yukata" itinerary you'll read elsewhere isn't currently possible — check the official bathhouse status page before you build a day around it.

The experience is the reason to go rather than the policy. You stay at a ryokan, you change into a yukata and wooden sandals, and you spend the evening walking between bathhouses along a willow-lined canal with a pass that gets you into all of them. It is the closest thing Japan has to a hot-spring pub crawl, and doing it without calculating who can see what is the entire point.

**One caveat, and it matters:** the town-wide policy covers the seven public baths, not the baths inside individual ryokan. Accommodations set their own rules for their own tubs. If your inn's private bath matters to you, ask when you book — and since Kinosaki ryokan almost all offer bookable private baths anyway, the fix is usually a single email.

### Beppu's municipal baths — ¥300, and no drama

Beppu, on Kyushu, produces more hot spring water than anywhere else in Japan and has the relaxed attitude of a place with too much of something to be precious about it. The city's own tourism arm publishes a map of over a hundred tattoo-friendly bathing spots, which is not a sentence any other Japanese city can write.

**Takegawara Onsen** is the one to see. It opened in 1879, the current building dates from 1938, and it looks like a temple that got into the bathing business. Three hundred yen gets you into a bath where the changing area and the tub are one continuous room with a staircase down into the water. There is also a sand bath, where an attendant buries you in volcanic sand while you lie there in a yukata reconsidering your assumptions about what counts as a spa treatment.

**Shibaseki Onsen** sits up in the hills in the Kannawa area, is also municipal, also ¥300, and is the quieter of the two. It's worth noting for one extra reason: it has family baths at around ¥2,000 per hour for up to four people. If you are traveling with someone who is nervous about the whole business regardless of tattoo policy, this is a facility where both answers exist under one roof.

**Hyotan Onsen** is the polished option — a day-use complex with a Michelin Green Guide star, fourteen private family baths, and a sand bath of its own. It appears on Beppu's tattoo-friendly list and is one of the few places where visitors report calling ahead and getting an unambiguous yes covering both the main baths and the sand bath. It also shows up in our [private onsen guide](/guides/private-onsen-japan-book-in-english/), which makes it a rare thing: somewhere you can bathe openly *or* privately depending on how you feel that morning.

### Tokyo: Daikoku-yu and Tokyo Yokujo

Central Tokyo is thin on real hot springs but thick with *sento* — neighborhood bathhouses — and the sento world has moved faster on tattoos than the resort world has.

**Daikoku-yu** in Sumida, a short walk from Skytree, is the standout because it isn't really a plain sento: it draws genuine hot-spring water and is registered as Oshiage Onsen. Its own site answers the tattoo question directly, in writing, in the affirmative. It has a rooftop open-air bath, and at ¥550 it is the cheapest legitimate onsen experience in Tokyo by a wide margin.

**Tokyo Yokujo** in Shinagawa earns its place for an unglamorous reason: it is the only facility in this entire guide whose published policy carries a visible date. Its FAQ entry stating that tattooed and *irezumi* guests may bathe was posted in June 2025. Every other policy page here is undated, which means every other policy here is technically a claim of unknown vintage. Yokujo also runs a reservable single-occupancy sauna, which is a useful thing to know if you want the sauna without the audience.

## Tier 2: Published, but Uncorroborated

Three more Tokyo bathhouses state the policy on their own websites in language that leaves no room for interpretation. What we could not find were independent accounts of tattooed guests bathing there recently. That's not a red flag — small neighborhood bathhouses generate little public commentary of any kind — but it is a weaker footing than Tier 1, and you deserve to know which is which.

- **Koganeyu** (Sumida) — a renovated sento with a craft beer tap and a DJ booth, which tells you most of what you need to know about its attitude. Its Q&A states that guests with tattoos may use the facility.
- **Mannenyu** (Shinjuku) — states that guests with *irezumi* and tattoos may bathe. Ordinary conduct and hygiene rules still apply, which is true everywhere and worth reading as reassurance rather than a catch.
- **Myohoyu** (Toshima) — the bluntest of the three: tattoos are no problem at all, you may bathe.

All three are ¥550. For a traveler based in Tokyo who wants a bath tonight rather than a trip, these are legitimate answers.

## Tier 3: Nothing Published, Consistently Fine

Two well-known places belong in a category most guides refuse to admit exists: no stated policy, but visitors keep describing the same outcome.

**Dogo Onsen Honkan** in Matsuyama is one of Japan's oldest and most famous bathhouses, the wooden labyrinth that helped inspire the bathhouse in *Spirited Away*. Its official site does not publish a tattoo policy either way. Visitor accounts through mid-2026 consistently describe tattooed bathers using it without incident, including people with full back pieces, and at least one traveler describes confirming by phone beforehand and being told it was fine.

**Kosugiyu** in Koenji is a beloved Tokyo sento with a strong local following. Same situation: no published policy, and consistent recent reports of visibly tattooed guests bathing normally, including traditional Japanese work.

Read this tier correctly. The absence of a published policy is not a hidden ban; plenty of businesses simply never wrote the page. But it also means nothing is promised to you at the door, and the person on shift that day has discretion. If either place is a centerpiece of your trip rather than a detour, spend the two minutes on a phone call. The script is below.

## Cover Stickers: What They Actually Do

Waterproof cover seals are sold at Don Quijote, most large drugstores and online, usually in skin-tone sheets you cut to size. They are genuinely useful and routinely oversold.

What they do: satisfy a facility whose rule is *no visible tattoos*, for a tattoo small enough to disappear under a patch roughly the size of a playing card. A wrist piece, a small ankle tattoo, a coin-sized design on a shoulder — these are exactly the cases stickers were made for, and staff at many facilities will hand you one if you ask.

What they don't do: cover a sleeve. Nothing sold in a drugstore covers a sleeve. They also don't help at a facility whose rule is *no tattooed guests* rather than *nothing visible* — the distinction sounds academic until you're standing at a counter, at which point it is the only thing that matters. And they loosen. Forty minutes in 41°C water with a scrubbing session in the middle is a hostile environment for adhesive; a seal that peels mid-bath is worse than no seal, because now it looks like concealment.

The honest summary: stickers are a tool for small tattoos at ambivalent facilities. If your work is large, skip this route entirely and use the list above or a private bath.

## The Geography of Easy and Hard

Where you go matters more than what you're covered in.

**Easy:** Kinosaki, by design. Beppu and much of Oita, by temperament. Tokyo's sento scene, increasingly. Osaka's larger bathing complexes, which serve a mixed international crowd and have mostly built defined paths for tattooed guests rather than blanket rules.

**Harder:** famous resort ryokan, especially high-end ones in Hakone, Kusatsu and Gero, where the guest base is domestic, older and conservative. Note the word *ryokan* there — Hakone's **day-use** baths are a different story, and a more welcoming one: Hakone Yuryo dropped its tattoo rules outright in April 2025, and Tenzan admits tattooed guests under an unusual one-per-group limit. We've costed all of them out in our [Hakone day trip guide](/guides/hakone-onsen-day-trip-from-tokyo/). Hotel spas attached to business hotels, which tend to copy whatever the chain's manual says. Public pools, gyms and some beaches — which are frequently *stricter* than hot springs, a fact that catches people out.

**Always available anywhere:** a private bath. This is the reason Route 3 exists.

## How to Check Any Facility Yourself

Lists go stale. Policies change in both directions, and this page will be wrong about something within a year — which is why the most valuable thing here is the method rather than the names.

**On the website.** Open the Japanese version, not the English one; the English page is marketing, the Japanese page is house rules. Look under 「よくあるご質問」 (FAQ) or 「ご利用案内」 (guide to use). Then search the page for 刺青 or タトゥー and read what surrounds it:

- 「入れ墨・タトゥーのある方はご遠慮ください」 — tattooed guests are asked to refrain. That's a no.
- 「タトゥーのある方もご入浴いただけます」 — tattooed guests may bathe. That's a yes.
- 「シールでお隠しください」 — please cover with a seal. That's a conditional yes.

Finding nothing means exactly that: nothing. It is not a yes and it is not a no, and it is the situation for both Tier 3 facilities above.

**On the phone.** One sentence does the job, and nobody expects your Japanese to be good:

> 刺青がありますが、入浴できますか？
> *Irezumi ga arimasu ga, nyūyoku dekimasu ka?*
> "I have a tattoo — may I bathe?"

If you'd rather not attempt it, the English word "tattoo" is universally understood in Japan, and "Tattoo, OK desu ka?" will be understood at any front desk in the country. You will get one of three answers: yes, no, or "small one is fine" — and all three are useful.

**Ask the right way.** Ask before you pay, not in the changing room. A front desk saying no costs you nothing; being asked to leave after undressing is a bad evening. And ask about the specific bath you want, since complexes often run different rules for the main bath, the sauna and the sand bath.

## If You're Turned Away

It will occasionally happen, and it is worth deciding in advance not to make it a scene.

The refusal is almost never personal and the person delivering it usually didn't write the rule. Say "wakarimashita" — understood — and go. Arguing has never once changed a Japanese front desk's mind and it makes the next tattooed traveler's welcome slightly worse.

Then fall back. Ask whether the facility has a *kashikiri* or *kazoku-buro* — a private bath — because a surprising number of places that won't let you in the main bath will happily rent you a room. If not, you're in a country with roughly three thousand hot spring resorts and the next one is close.

## FAQ

**Are tattoos illegal in Japan?**
No. There is no law against having them, showing them, or getting them. Every bathing restriction you'll encounter is a private business's own rule, which is why they vary so much from one door to the next.

**Will a small tattoo really get me turned away?**
At a strict facility, sometimes yes — the rule is often written as "any visible tattoo" and staff apply it literally. At most places a small piece is either fine or fixed with a sticker. The distinction to listen for is whether the rule is about visibility or about you.

**Do cover stickers actually work?**
For small tattoos at facilities whose rule is about visibility, yes, and staff will often supply them. For large work, no — nothing sold in a drugstore covers a sleeve, and a seal that peels mid-soak reads worse than an uncovered tattoo.

**Is Kinosaki really fine with a full back piece or sleeves?**
Yes, for the seven public bathhouses — the stated policy is any size, nothing covered, and visitors describe full traditional work being bathed openly there through 2026. Individual ryokan set their own rules for their own baths, so confirm that separately when you book.

**Are gyms, pools and beaches the same as onsen?**
Often stricter, which surprises people. Municipal pools and gym facilities frequently enforce blanket tattoo bans long after nearby bathhouses have relaxed. Don't assume an onsen-friendly city is friendly everywhere.

**What about a ryokan room with its own bath?**
That's the cleanest solution in existence and no policy applies, because the bath is inside your room. It costs accordingly. A bookable private bath at a normal ryokan gets you most of the benefit for a fraction of the price.

**Could I be asked to leave after I've already gotten in?**
It's rare and it's usually a result of not asking at the desk first. Facilities would overwhelmingly rather decline you at the counter than manage a situation at the tub, which is precisely why asking before you pay is the whole technique.

## The Bottom Line

The tattoo question in Japan is smaller than the internet makes it and more specific than the listicles admit. There is no law, there is no national policy, and there is a real and growing set of places that decided to say yes in writing.

Start with **Kinosaki** if you want the full onsen-town experience without thinking about it once. Take **Beppu** if you want to bathe in a 1938 temple of a bathhouse for ¥300. Use **Daikoku-yu** or **Tokyo Yokujo** if you're in Tokyo and want a bath tonight. Treat **Dogo Honkan** and **Kosugiyu** as excellent but worth a phone call. And keep a [private bath](/guides/private-onsen-japan-book-in-english/) in reserve for everywhere else.

Policies published on this page were checked against the facilities' own sites in August 2026, and cross-checked against recent visitor accounts. Where the two disagreed or one was missing, we said so above rather than rounding it up to a yes.
