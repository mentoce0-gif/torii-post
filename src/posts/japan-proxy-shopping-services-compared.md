## How Proxy Shopping Actually Works (60-Second Version)

Every service in this list follows the same basic flow:

1. **You find an item** on a Japanese site (or search directly inside the proxy's own portal, which is easier).
2. **The proxy buys it** using their Japanese address and payment method. You pay the item price + their service fee.
3. **It arrives at their warehouse**, usually within 2–5 days. Most services photograph it on request.
4. **You choose international shipping** — and this is where consolidation matters. If you've bought five items, shipping them in one box instead of five can cut your shipping bill in half.
5. **It ships to you.** Expect 4–10 days for express options (DHL, FedEx, EMS) or 1–3 months for surface mail.

The service fee is only part of the picture. Consolidation policy, domestic shipping handling, and currency conversion rates are where the sneaky costs hide. Keep that in mind as you read the reviews below.

## The Four Hidden Costs That Decide Your Real Total

Before we get into the individual reviews, let me save you from the mistakes I made in my first year of proxy shopping. The advertised service fee is the *least* important number. These four costs are what actually separate a good deal from a bad one:

**1. Domestic shipping.** Every item has to travel from the seller to the proxy's warehouse first, and within Japan that typically costs ¥500–1,000 per shipment. Buy five items from five different sellers and you could rack up ¥4,000 in domestic shipping before your haul even leaves Japan. This is the single most common "wait, why is my invoice so high?" moment for beginners. (It's also why Neokyo bundling domestic shipping into its flat fee is a bigger deal than it sounds.)

**2. Dimensional weight.** International carriers charge by actual weight *or* box volume, whichever is greater. A big box of light plushies gets billed like a box of bricks. Packing quality varies noticeably between services — a snug repack can save you thousands of yen on a large order, and an oversized box can silently erase every yen you saved on fees.

**3. Currency conversion.** Some services apply their own exchange rate with a built-in spread of 2–4% above the market rate. On a ¥50,000 order, that's up to ¥2,000 vanishing invisibly. Paying in yen with a no-foreign-fee credit card, where the service allows it, is usually the cleanest option.

**4. Storage deadlines.** Free storage windows range from 30 to 60 days depending on the service. That's plenty for normal shopping — until you're holding three items waiting for a pre-order that ships next season. Miss the deadline and you're either paying storage fees or forced to ship a half-empty box.

None of these are scams; they're just the physics and economics of moving boxes across the ocean. But once you know to check all four, comparing services gets a lot less confusing.

Here's why that combination matters. Most proxy shopping isn't one big purchase — it's a handful of smaller ones. A ¥2,000 manga lot here, a ¥6,000 figure there, a ¥1,500 CD from a different seller. With ZenMarket, that's ¥900 in fees total, and all three items go into one box for free. The savings on international shipping alone (typically 30–50% versus shipping separately) usually dwarf the fees.

The buying experience is also the smoothest of the flat-fee services. Their site has integrated search for Yahoo! The ¥300 flat fee also stings on very cheap items — paying ¥300 on a ¥500 keychain is a 60% surcharge. And their customer support, while polite, can be slow during big sale seasons.

**Choose ZenMarket if:** you buy multiple items per month, you bid on Yahoo! Auctions, or you want one service that handles 90% of situations well.

## 2. Buyee — Best for Cheap Single Items and Mercari

Buyee is the biggest name in the game, and it has one structural advantage nobody can match: **it's the official proxy partner of Mercari Japan.** Mercari listings literally show a "Buy with Buyee" button. That integration means faster processing and fewer cancelled orders on Mercari than any competitor.

Buyee's fee structure changed to a 6% commission model, and that changes the math significantly. On a ¥3,000 item, 6% is just ¥180 — cheaper than every flat-fee competitor. On a ¥30,000 item, it's ¥1,800 — six times ZenMarket's fee. Buyee is now clearly optimized for high-volume, low-price shopping.

The platform itself is polished, with apps that actually work, frequent coupon campaigns (their shipping discount coupons can be genuinely substantial), and the widest marketplace coverage in the industry.

**The downsides:** Consolidation costs ¥500 per package, and their packing has a reputation for being generously — let's say *spaciously* — boxed, which inflates dimensional weight charges. Various small fees (payment processing, options like protective packaging) accumulate. For a multi-item haul of mid-priced goods, Buyee usually ends up the most expensive option on this list.

**Choose Buyee if:** you mostly buy individual items under ¥5,000, you shop primarily on Mercari, or you want to stack their discount coupons on a one-off purchase.

## 3. FROM JAPAN — Best for Big-Ticket Purchases

FROM JAPAN has been around since the early days of proxy shopping, and its fee structure hides a gem: **¥300 per item, but capped at ¥800 for any order from the same store.** Buy ten items from one Surugaya listing spree and you pay ¥800 in fees, total.

The cap also makes FROM JAPAN the mathematically cheapest option for expensive single items. Since there's no percentage fee, a ¥50,000 watch costs you ¥300 in service fees — versus ¥3,000 on Buyee's 6% model. As a rule of thumb from my own spreadsheets: **above roughly ¥14,000 per item, FROM JAPAN wins on fees.**

Their "Buy for me" concierge option is also worth knowing about. For listings that are confusing, bundle-only, or require seller communication, their staff will handle the back-and-forth in Japanese. It's slower, but it opens doors the automated services can't.

**The downsides:** The two-step payment system (pay for the item first, then pay fees + shipping later) confuses first-timers and means two separate currency conversions. The website feels a generation older than ZenMarket's or Buyee's. Processing can be slower, which matters for auction wins with impatient sellers.

**Choose FROM JAPAN if:** you're buying items over ¥14,000, ordering many items from a single store, or need human help with a tricky listing.

## 4. Neokyo — Best for Predictable Budgeting

Neokyo is the smallest service on this list, but it's earned a loyal following among figure and doujin collectors for one reason: **radical fee transparency.** The ¥350 per-item fee *includes* domestic shipping to their warehouse — a cost that every other service passes through separately and that catches beginners off guard constantly.

That means when you're calculating a Neokyo order, the math is genuinely simple: item price + ¥350 + international shipping. No surprise ¥800 domestic shipping charge appearing after purchase. For budget-conscious buyers who hate fee anxiety, this is worth a lot. Consolidation is free, with a ¥500 packing fee for packages under 2kg.

Their support team is small but responsive, and the whole service has a pleasant "run by people who actually collect things" energy.

**The downsides:** The marketplace search is less polished than the big players', with rougher translations. Some payment options are limited. And because the team is small, processing times can stretch during Golden Week or year-end sale rushes. No real concierge service for complicated purchases.

**Choose Neokyo if:** you want zero fee surprises, you're a collector making regular small-to-medium hauls, or you've been burned by hidden domestic shipping charges elsewhere.

## 5. Japan Rabbit — Best for the Impossible Purchases

Japan Rabbit (you may know it by its old name, White Rabbit Express) is a different animal entirely. It's not a self-service platform — it's a concierge. You send them a link, or even just a description, and a human in Tokyo figures out how to get it.

That includes stores that actively block proxy services, fan club exclusives, lottery items, in-store pickups, and purchases that require phone calls or negotiation in Japanese. I've seen them pull off purchases that every automated service flat-out rejected.

Pricing is quote-based and unapologetically premium. You're paying for human time, and it shows in both the cost and the quality — packing is excellent, communication is in fluent English, and edge cases get solved instead of auto-cancelled.

**The downsides:** cost, obviously. For routine Mercari or Rakuten purchases, using Japan Rabbit is like hiring a private chef to make toast. This is a specialist tool.

**Choose Japan Rabbit if:** the item is rare, the store blocks proxies, or the purchase requires a human being on the ground in Japan.

## Which International Shipping Method Should You Pick?

Once your box is packed, every service offers roughly the same menu of carriers, and the right choice depends on what's inside:

- **DHL / FedEx express** — 3–7 days, full tracking, best for anything valuable or fragile. Costs the most upfront but handles customs paperwork smoothly, which can actually make it *cheaper* in tears and delays for high-value orders.
- **EMS (Japan Post express)** — the classic middle option: 4–10 days to most countries, solid tracking, decent insurance, usually cheaper than DHL for small-to-medium boxes.
- **Airmail / small packet** — 1–3 weeks, fine for books, CDs, and anything under 2kg that won't break your heart if it dawdles.
- **Surface (sea) mail** — 1–3 months, but often a third of the airmail price. Genuinely great for heavy, non-urgent hauls like manga box sets. I've sent 15kg of books by sea for less than a 5kg airmail box would have cost.

One practical tip: don't default to the fastest option out of nerves. For a typical figure or clothing order, EMS hits the sweet spot of speed, cost, and insurance coverage almost every time.

## FAQ

**Is proxy shopping from Japan legit, or will I get scammed?**
The five services above are all established companies that have operated for years and processed millions of orders. The genuine risks are item condition on secondhand marketplaces (read listings carefully, request warehouse photos) and customs charges on your end — not the proxies themselves.

**How much does shipping from Japan actually cost?**
As a rough guide in 2026: a 1kg box runs about ¥2,500–4,500 via airmail-class options and ¥5,000+ via DHL/FedEx express, depending on destination. This is exactly why consolidation matters more than service fees for multi-item orders.

**Do I have to pay customs duties?**
Depends on your country's de minimis threshold. US buyers currently face duties on most shipments, EU buyers pay VAT on virtually everything, and thresholds change — check your country's current rules before a big order rather than relying on year-old forum posts.

**Which service is cheapest overall?**
For items under ¥5,000: Buyee. For ¥5,000–14,000 or any multi-item haul: ZenMarket. Above ¥14,000 or bulk orders from one store: FROM JAPAN. There is no single cheapest service — it genuinely depends on your cart.

**Can proxies buy pre-orders and lottery items?**
Pre-orders: yes, all the major services handle them, though your storage clock and payment timing vary. Lottery (chuusen) items and fan club exclusives: usually only via concierge services like Japan Rabbit.

**How long does the whole process take?**
Typical timeline: 2–5 days for the item to reach the warehouse, then 4–10 days international express shipping. Budget two to three weeks door-to-door for a normal order, longer if you're consolidating multiple purchases.

**Can I use a proxy to buy from Amazon Japan?**
Usually you don't need to — Amazon Japan ships many items internationally itself, and its English interface is decent. Proxies earn their keep on the marketplaces that *don't* ship overseas: Mercari, Yahoo! Auctions, Rakuten sellers, Surugaya, and small specialty shops. Check Amazon's own international shipping first and save the proxy fee when you can.

**What happens if my item arrives at the warehouse damaged or wrong?**
This is where service quality really shows. All five services will photograph items on request (sometimes for a small fee) — use that option for anything secondhand or fragile. If a marketplace seller misrepresented an item, the proxy can usually open a dispute on your behalf, but windows are short: check your warehouse photos within a few days, not weeks. For genuinely valuable purchases, this dispute support is a strong argument for concierge-style services.
