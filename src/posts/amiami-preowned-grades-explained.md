You've found the figure. It's been sold out for two years, aftermarket prices are silly, and there it is on AmiAmi's pre-owned section for 40% under market — labeled *"Pre-owned ITEM:B / BOX:B."*

And now you're doing what every collector does the first time: hovering over the buy button, wondering what horrors "B" might be hiding. Scratches? Sun damage? A mysterious smell?

I've bought a lot of pre-owned figures from AmiAmi over the years, across almost every grade they assign, and here's the short version: **AmiAmi's grades are conservative to a degree that regularly surprises Western buyers.** A "B" from AmiAmi routinely arrives in condition that eBay sellers would confidently list as "like new." But the system has real quirks — including one genuine trap around box grades — and knowing them is the difference between bargain hunting with confidence and gambling.

This guide decodes every grade, explains what actually shows up at your door, and covers what to do on the rare occasion something arrives worse than described.

## First, How AmiAmi's Pre-Owned Section Actually Works

Context matters here, because AmiAmi's pre-owned pipeline is *why* the grades are trustworthy.

AmiAmi buys figures back directly from collectors in Japan — that's the only source. Every item passes through their staff, who inspect it, screen it for bootlegs, grade it, and note any significant damage on the product page. You are not buying from a random marketplace seller; you're buying from AmiAmi's own inspected inventory. This is fundamentally different from taking your chances with an anonymous listing on eBay or Mercari.

Two consequences of this pipeline are worth knowing:

**The bootleg risk is close to zero.** Their inspection staff handle thousands of authentic figures; fakes get filtered out. I'd rate the practical bootleg risk on AmiAmi pre-owned as lower than almost anywhere else you can buy secondhand figures, including some Western retailers.

**The grading reflects Japanese collector standards.** The figures come from Japanese collectors, many of whom keep items boxed, displayed briefly in glass cabinets away from sunlight, or never opened at all. The baseline condition of the supply is simply higher than the average Western collection, and the graders evaluate against strict domestic expectations.

## Decoding the Format: ITEM vs BOX

Every pre-owned listing shows two separate ratings in the product title:

> Pre-owned (ITEM:B / BOX:B)

- **ITEM** = the figure itself: paint, parts, joints, accessories.
- **BOX** = only the packaging: the outer cardboard box, window film, and so on.

These are graded independently, and they matter to completely different people. If you display your figures and toss the boxes in a closet, the BOX grade is nearly irrelevant to you — and ignoring it unlocks the best deals on the entire site. If you keep everything mint-in-box for resale value, read the box section below carefully, because that's where the system gets weird.

## The Grades, From Best to Worst

Here's the official meaning of each grade — and, more usefully, what my orders and the collective experience of the collector community say actually arrives.

### S — New

AmiAmi's top pre-owned grade: guaranteed unused and unopened. Functionally a new figure that happens to have passed through someone else's hands, usually of something now sold out — which is the entire appeal.

**What actually arrives:** a new figure. The only caveat is that "unopened" says nothing about the box condition — check the BOX grade if shelf presentation matters to you.

### A — Like New, Sealed

The maker's seal is intact, or the item is otherwise unopened as far as AmiAmi can tell. Note that this is **not** the "opened but tidy" grade that a lot of English guides describe it as — including, until August 2026, this one.

**What actually arrives:** a new figure, same as S in practice.

### A− — Like New, Unsealed

Not sealed by the maker, but shows every sign of never having been opened. This is the grade that does the work most people think A does.

**What actually arrives:** indistinguishable from new in nearly every case. The discount over new is usually modest, and for sold-out items this is the safest possible secondhand buy.

### B+ — Practically Like New

The grade AmiAmi uses when an item is essentially flawless but they can't quite justify an A — maybe it was clearly displayed for a while, maybe the accessories bag was unsealed.

**What actually arrives:** community consensus, which matches my experience exactly, is that B+ looks like new. If you can find any defect at all, it will take deliberate searching under strong light. B+ items are, for my money, the single best value-to-risk ratio on the site.

### B — Minor Wear (The Sweet Spot, With an Asterisk)

Here's the grade that scares everyone, and shouldn't. Officially it means visible minor wear or damage is possible: small paint transfers, light dust, tiny marks.

**What actually arrives:** the overwhelming majority of B items arrive looking new or near-new. Long-running community threads on this are almost comical — thread after thread of "I nervously ordered a B/B and it was flawless." One collector who documented dozens of B purchases reported finding genuine (still small) flaws on roughly two out of thirty figures. That matches my own hit rate almost exactly: most Bs are indistinguishable from new; occasionally one has a faint paint transfer or a tiny scuff you'd need to hunt for.

The asterisk: B is also the widest grade, and *sometimes* — rarely — a B item has a flaw that's real, like a visible-at-arm's-length paint rub. If a specific defect were significant, AmiAmi would typically note it in the listing or bump the item to C. No note usually means nothing worth noting.

**Buy B if:** you display your figures and you like saving 30–50%. Skip B if you're the kind of collector who inspects paint edges with a loupe and loses sleep over a dust particle.

### C — Real Flaws, Honestly Disclosed

C is where "pre-owned" starts meaning what Westerners expect it to mean. Expect a genuine flaw: discoloration, a paint chip, a loose joint, a missing minor accessory. The crucial difference from B: **for C-grade items, AmiAmi describes the specific damage on the product page.** Read that note — it tells you exactly what you're accepting, and the discounts are steep.

**What actually arrives:** the flaw described, and usually not more. Collectors regularly report the described damage being *less* noticeable in person than the warning suggested — "slight discoloration on the knees" that you can't see from two feet away. C items are genuine bargains for display collectors who don't mind the specific documented flaw.

### J — Junk

Significant damage, missing parts, broken pieces — sold explicitly as-is, mostly for parts harvesters, repainters, and customizers. You will know what you're getting into; the listing says so plainly. Not for normal collecting, excellent for kitbashing raw material.

## The One Genuine Trap: BOX Grades

Now for the quirk that actually catches people. Item grading is conservative and reliable. **Box grading is vaguer, and the community has noticed for years that a "B" box is sometimes worse than a "C" box.**

Two reasons. First, unlike C-grade *items*, AmiAmi generally doesn't write damage descriptions for C-grade *boxes* — a C box might be lightly creased, or torn, or crushed, or missing its window film; you won't know which until it arrives. Second, box condition genuinely matters less to the grading process, so the line between box grades is blurrier.

**There is also a BOX:N, and it means there is no box at all.** It's easy to skim past when you're reading the item grade, and no amount of ITEM:A makes up for it if the packaging was the point.

Practical guidance:

- **Display collectors:** ignore box grades entirely. This is your discount lever — an ITEM:A/BOX:C listing is often dramatically cheaper for a figure in perfect condition.
- **Mint-in-box collectors:** treat any box grade below B+ as a coin flip, check for BOX:N before anything else, and consider paying up for an S or A listing instead.
- **One edge case worth knowing:** a severely damaged box can occasionally compromise protection in transit. It's rare — AmiAmi packs well — but if you're ordering a heavy scale figure with a BOX:C, understand the inner blister is doing all the work.

## The Other Thing Nobody Tells You: There Are No Individual Photos

Here's a policy that surprises almost every first-time buyer: **the photos on a pre-owned listing are stock promotional images, not photos of the actual item you're buying — and AmiAmi will not photograph the individual item for you, even if you ask.** Their support pages are explicit about this, and they also won't explain why a particular item received its grade.

Coming from eBay culture, where you zoom into twelve blurry photos of the actual box corner, this feels alarming. In practice, it's the flip side of the system that makes AmiAmi work: they process enormous volumes of buyback stock, and per-item photography would slow the pipeline and raise prices. Instead of photos, you're given a grade assigned by a professional inspector with consistent standards — plus written damage notes whenever something crosses into C territory.

How to shop comfortably inside this policy:

- **Treat the grade as the photo.** A thousand community unboxings have effectively calibrated what each grade looks like; that calibration is what this article is for.
- **Read the "Condition" notes on every listing.** When staff felt something was worth mentioning, they wrote it down. Absence of notes on an A/B item is itself information.
- **Let the price be your tiebreaker.** Two copies of the same figure at B and B+ often sit a few hundred yen apart. When in doubt, the small premium for B+ buys you a meaningful bump in certainty.
- **If photo-level certainty is non-negotiable,** the domestic marketplaces (Mercari Japan, Surugaya) via proxy are your alternative — seller photos of the actual item, in exchange for no professional grading and no bootleg screening. It's a genuine trade-off, not a strict upgrade.

## Why AmiAmi Grades So Conservatively

This isn't altruism; it's incentive design, and it works in your favor. AmiAmi's dispute process is slow and manual — if you report a misgraded item, they request photos, review internally for about a week, and if they agree it was their error, compensate with points. (Collectors report figures clustering around 600–800 points, roughly ¥600–800; AmiAmi publishes no compensation schedule, so treat that as anecdote rather than entitlement.) That process is expensive for them and mildly unsatisfying for you.

The cheapest way for AmiAmi to avoid it is to under-promise: grade a like-new figure as B, so almost every customer is pleasantly surprised and disputes stay rare. That's exactly the pattern thousands of orders confirm. When a company's lazy path and your interests point the same direction, you can relax a little.

## My Actual Buying Strategy, Grade by Grade

After years of doing this, here's the decision framework I actually use:

1. **Sold-out grail at a fair price?** Buy at S, A, A− or B+ without hesitation. These are new figures wearing pessimistic labels.
2. **Display figure, best value?** B is the sweet spot. Expect ~90% odds of near-mint, accept the small chance of a minor cosmetic flaw you'll forget about in a week.
3. **Deep discount hunting?** Read C listings carefully. If the described flaw is somewhere invisible (back of the base, underside, box-only issues), these are the best prices on authentic figures anywhere on the internet.
4. **MIB/resale collecting?** Only S/A with BOX:A or B+, and accept that pre-owned may not be your market.
5. **Never** buy J unless you own hobby knives and know why.

One more habit worth stealing: pre-owned stock is one-of-a-kind. When a well-priced B+ of a rare figure appears, it's gone in hours. Decide your personal grade threshold *before* the figure you want shows up, so you can act instead of deliberating while someone in another timezone checks out your cart.

## A Worked Example: Reading a Real Listing

Let's put it all together on a typical listing you might actually face:

> **Nendoroid [Popular Character] — Pre-owned (ITEM:B / BOX:C)** — ¥4,280
> *Condition note: none. New copies sold out; aftermarket price ¥7,500+.*

Walk through it like a practiced buyer:

1. **ITEM:B, no condition note.** Statistically this arrives near-mint. If something specific were wrong — a missing accessory, a visible paint chip — the note or a C grade would say so. Nendoroids also ship with an inner blister that protects the figure regardless of box state.
2. **BOX:C, no description.** This is the coin-flip part: could be a crushed corner, could be a torn window. If the box goes in a closet, this is exactly the flaw you *want* to be discounted for.
3. **The price is 43% under aftermarket** for a figure whose flaw, in all likelihood, is cardboard.

For a display collector, this listing is close to free money. For a mint-in-box collector, it's an easy pass. Same listing, opposite verdicts — which is the entire point of learning the system: the grades don't tell you whether to buy, they tell you *what you're being paid to tolerate.*

One timing note: pre-owned stock arrives in waves, often appearing in batches when buyback processing clears. If you're hunting something specific, checking the newly-listed pre-owned feed regularly beats refreshing a single product page — and popular items at fair prices genuinely do disappear within hours.

## When the Figure Sells Out Anyway: Your Plan B

Because every pre-owned listing is a single physical item, you will eventually lose one. When that happens, the same figure is usually floating around Japan's domestic secondhand market — Mercari Japan, Yahoo! Auctions, Surugaya — at comparable or better prices. ## FAQ

**Is AmiAmi pre-owned legit? Are the figures authentic?**
Yes. Items are bought back from collectors inside Japan and inspected by AmiAmi staff before listing. The practical bootleg risk is about as close to zero as secondhand figure buying gets.

**What does "Pre-owned ITEM:B/BOX:B" mean?**
The figure itself grades B (minor wear possible, usually arrives near-mint) and the box also grades B (light shelf wear). Item and box are graded separately — decide which one you actually care about.

**Is B grade worth it, honestly?**
For display collectors, almost always. The community's collective experience — and mine — is that the large majority of B items are indistinguishable from new, at 30–50% off. The occasional exception has a minor cosmetic flaw, not damage.

**Why is a pre-owned figure sometimes MORE expensive than its original price?**
Because the figure sold out years ago and the pre-owned market has repriced it. AmiAmi prices pre-owned stock against the current aftermarket, not the original MSRP. It can still be the cheapest authentic copy available anywhere.

**What if my item arrives worse than its grade?**
Contact AmiAmi with photos promptly. They review internally (expect about a week) and, where they accept the error, have compensated collectors with store points in the several-hundred-yen range. There is no published policy setting that amount, so don't treat it as a guaranteed remedy. It's not a generous process, but you'll rarely need it — misgrades in the harsh direction are genuinely uncommon.

**Do pre-owned purchases earn points or count toward shipping like new items?**
They ship from the same warehouse and combine with in-stock new items in one box, which is a quiet bonus: pad a pre-owned bargain order with supplies or small in-stock items and save on shipping per item.

**How fast does pre-owned stock move?**
Fast. Each listing is one physical unit, and well-priced B+ copies of popular figures routinely vanish within hours of listing. Check the pre-owned section at consistent times (new stock tends to appear in batches) if you're hunting something specific.

## The Bottom Line

AmiAmi's pre-owned grades are that rare thing on the internet: a rating system biased in the *buyer's* favor. S, A and A− are new figures in practice, B+ is the best value-to-risk ratio on the site, B is a bargain with a small asterisk, and C tells you honestly what's wrong before you pay. The only real trap is trusting box grades if mint packaging matters to you.

Set your grade threshold, ignore the box rating if you're a display collector, and that two-years-sold-out grail suddenly costs 40% less than everyone else is paying for it.
